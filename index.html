whybox

A guessing game that is quietly a lesson in how experiments work. Ten minutes, nothing to install, and no background knowledge needed.

What you do

  1. Type a code 24 letters long, using only the letters A C G T. For example AAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAA.
  2. Press Test it. A machine gives your code a score between 0 and 6. Higher is better.
  3. Nobody tells you how the score is worked out. Figuring that out is the whole game.

You get 15 tries. Here is the thing worth watching for: scoring well is easy, and you will probably hit the top score by accident in your first few goes. Being able to say why it is high is much harder. That gap between doing well and understanding is what this is really about.

The rule was made up for this game. It is not real biology, so knowing biology will not help you, and not knowing any will not hold you back. Everybody starts level.

Your notes

Two questions, not one. Most people only ever ask the first, and that is the single most common reason people get stuck on puzzles like this one.

Read this after you have played

What was the point of that?

Real experimental science looks a lot like what you just did. Nature does not hand over its rules. You poke at something, write down what comes back, guess at the pattern, and poke again in a way that would show you up if your guess is wrong. The last part is the one people skip.

Notice what happened to your two numbers. If you got a high score early and still could not describe the rule, you found the thing this whole project is built around: doing well and understanding are not the same, and from the outside they look identical. That matters well beyond a browser game. It is the reason a computer model can predict something accurately while telling you nothing about why, and the reason an AI system can top a leaderboard without having understood the problem at all.

The three parts of a setup like this

Any version of this game has the same three pieces. The things you submit, here a 24-letter code. The measurement that gets made on them. And the sealed machine that runs the measurement and hands back a number, usually called an oracle. Keeping the oracle shut is what forces you to work the rule out rather than look it up.

Oracles come in three kinds. A wet one runs a genuine experiment in a lab: real, slow, expensive, and nobody knows the right answer in advance. A damp one runs a computer model trained on real measurements: much faster, and wrong in whatever ways the model is wrong. A dry one, like the one you just played against, runs a rule somebody invented. There is no biology in it, so nothing you found is a discovery about the world. In exchange, the answer is known exactly, which is the only reason your description could be checked at all.

Two things we are not pretending about

Nothing is really hidden in a web page. This page runs on your own computer, so somebody who knows where to look can read the rule straight out of it. That is how browsers work and we did not find a way around it. What the fingerprint does protect is narrower and still worth having: the rule could not change while you played.

The scoring of your description is crude. See the warning above. It is published with its own weakness measured rather than quietly hoped over.

If you want to go further

This page is one puzzle with one player. The full version is a Python package that runs the same thing with a proper lab notebook, automatic opponents that chase a high score without ever understanding anything, and grading that keeps the two numbers apart on purpose. It has no dependencies, so there is no setup to go wrong.

git clone https://github.com/phagehunter/whybox.git
cd whybox
python3 -m whybox run codon-lite --agent human --rounds 15

You can also write your own puzzle. The only real requirement is a system where you know the rule and the player does not. The guide is short, and the interesting puzzles are the ones where a clever player fails in a way you can explain.
