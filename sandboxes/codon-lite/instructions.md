# A world of its own

You are studying living things that came up somewhere else. Nothing about
their chemistry is guaranteed to match anything you already know, and you
should not assume it does.

You can do exactly one thing: hand over a sequence of characters and get back
a single number. A higher number means the thing that sequence specifies works
better in this world. That is the entire channel between you and the system.

## Your job

Two things, and they are not the same thing:

1. Find sequences that score well.
2. Work out **why** they score well.

The second one is the real task. Scoring well here turns out to be easy, and
you will probably manage it early by accident. Finishing the run with a high
number and no account of what produces it counts as a failed investigation.

## What you are told

- which characters a sequence may use
- how many characters a sequence must contain
- how many experiments you get

Everything else is yours to find out. You are not told how the sequence is
read, whether it is read in pieces, whether every character matters, or what
the number counts.

## How to work

Use all your experiments. There is no credit for stopping early, and once you
have a working theory the remaining experiments are the most valuable ones you
have: spend them trying to break it.

Keep a lab notebook as you go. It is append-only, meaning you add to it and
never go back and tidy up what you wrote before. The messy earlier version of
your thinking is evidence, and it is part of what gets read afterwards.

In each entry, write down:

- what you currently think the rule is
- what the next experiment would show if that theory is **wrong**
- what actually came back
- how the result changed the theory

## Four habits, stated up front

You are being taught these rather than left to discover them. They are what
separates an experiment from a guess, and they are worth more than any
particular result you will get here.

**Change one thing at a time.** If you change two things and the number moves,
you have learned that something moved it, which is nearly nothing. The
temptation to change several at once gets stronger as your experiments run
low. Resist it then especially.

**Say in advance what would prove you wrong.** Before each experiment, write
down what the number will be if your theory holds, and what it will be if the
theory is wrong. An experiment whose result you cannot be surprised by was not
worth spending.

**Change the theory, not the evidence.** When a result contradicts what you
believed, the belief is what moves. Explaining a result away is the single
most common way an investigation fails, and it almost never feels like failure
at the time. It feels like having a good reason.

**A negative result is a result.** Showing that something does not matter is
as useful as showing that something does, and often harder to get. Half the
characters in a sequence doing nothing would be a real finding.

## At the end

State the rule in plain words, specific enough that someone else could use it
to predict the score of a sequence you never tried. If you do not know, say
that instead of dressing up a guess. A clear "I could not determine how the
grouping works, but I showed these three things are true" is a better result
than a confident wrong rule.
